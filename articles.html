Influencing Without Authority

What Florence Nightingale teaches us about clarity, evidence and moving a room when the title is not yours.

Click to expand / collapse full text ▾
“Nightingale did not out-rank the colonel. She out-clarified him.”

She had no title. No budget. No authority. Just a notebook, three coloured pens, and a hospital full of dying soldiers nobody would listen to her about.

At a session with Prof. Rakesh Godhwani at IIM Bangalore, I learned the deeper story behind Florence Nightingale—not only the lady with the lamp, but a woman who used data visualisation to save lives.

The evidence changed the conversation

In 1854, during the Crimean War, Nightingale arrived at a military hospital in Turkey with 25 nurses and almost no formal authority. Soldiers were dying less from battle wounds than from infections spreading through filthy, poorly managed wards. When her requests to improve sanitation were dismissed, she changed her method.

She collected data for months: injuries, infections and deaths. Then she translated it into a hand-drawn polar-area chart—the form now associated with her famous rose diagrams. The visual made a complex pattern immediately legible. Evidence accomplished what repeated pleading could not.

  • Clarity of thought
  • Clarity of expression
  • Presence
  • Connection

Position is not persuasion

Prof. Godhwani framed influence without authority through four pillars: clarity of thought, expression, presence and connection. Nightingale did not need a higher rank to move the system; she needed a clearer case.

We often assume that an idea needs a title behind it before it can move a room. Nightingale had neither position nor budget, yet her work influenced hospital practice at a national scale. The lesson is simple: when authority is unavailable, make the problem impossible to misunderstand.

Essay 02 · AI & Strategy 13 min read · 16 July 2026

Digital Sovereignty in the Age of AI

The invisible dependency that could define the next global power shift—and why resilience matters more than isolation.

Click to expand / collapse full text ▾
“The goal is not disconnection. It is avoiding total dependence on infrastructure you do not control and cannot replace.”

The sovereignty we quietly traded away

Imagine a ministry unable to open documents, a public-sector bank unable to process transactions, or a hospital locked out of its scheduling system—not because of a cyberattack, but because a foreign provider changed the commercial or geopolitical terms of access. Dramatic? Yes. Impossible? Less so than it sounds.

Over two decades, governments and businesses moved to global cloud platforms for good reasons: lower costs, faster innovation, strong security and continuous updates. The problem is that better gradually became irreplaceable. Microsoft, Salesforce, Google Workspace, AWS, Azure, GCP and WhatsApp now form part of the operating environment of modern economies.

Technology is geopolitics

Semiconductor export restrictions, GPU-allocation constraints and telecom-equipment bans show that technology supply chains can become foreign-policy instruments. The exposure exists even if an enterprise-software cutoff never occurs. Dependency itself is the vulnerability.

The strategic question is not whether global platforms are good or bad. It is whether a country can continue governing, banking, treating patients and running businesses if access changes suddenly.

India is building more than chatbots

Krutrim has moved toward cloud services and GPU infrastructure. Sarvam AI is building multilingual models, speech systems and enterprise deployments. Neysa is focusing on domestic AI-cloud infrastructure. Together they illustrate three different pieces of a sovereign stack: models, interfaces and compute.

Essay 03 · Healthcare 8 min read · 2026

Healthcare AI: Build Fast, Scale Safely

Risk-based regulation, clinical co-creation and designing for the 99%—a field note on trustworthy healthcare AI.

Click to expand / collapse full text ▾
“In healthcare, regulation should be the runway that lets innovation take off safely.”

Scale requires trust

Healthcare is shifting from AI experimentation to deployment at scale. But scale requires trust, and trust requires governance. The EU AI Act categorises systems by risk rather than treating every algorithm as equally dangerous.

Design for low-resource settings

Healthcare AI should not become a collection of shiny objects built only for top-tier hospitals. Its deepest value may emerge in low-resource settings: screening, triage and simple decision support for frontline workers.

  • Triage over autonomous treatment
  • Simple decision support for frontline workers
  • Supervised use by trained professionals
  • Compatibility with digital public infrastructure
